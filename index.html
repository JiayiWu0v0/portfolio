Dog Cafés Become Urban Escapes as Mental Health Market Grows

By Jiayi Wu

When Wang Ying opened her dog café, Two Puppies, in Beijing in June 2025, she didn’t expect it to become a weekday refuge for everyone.

“I started this café by chance,” Wang said. “One day after work, I walked my two dogs and wanted a drink, but nearby pubs didn’t allow me in. All said the dogs might disturb other customers. So my original goal was to give dog walkers the dignity to have a drink.”

Two dogs play with each other freely at Two Puppies on June 10. Photo by Wu Jiayi.

China’s pet economy is rapidly expanding. The 2025 Pet Industry Consumption Trends Insight shows that in 2024, the urban pet adoption market size exceeded 300 billion yuan, with the number of pet cats and dogs rebounding steadily. In addition, the study reported that in 2023, 190 new enterprises with 'pet' in their business scope were registered nationwide. In terms of regional distribution, Beijing ranks first in pet ownership among Chinese cities.

At the same time, the number of people diagnosed with mental health conditions is increasing, particularly depression and anxiety. An article published in The British Journal of Psychiatry revealed that from 1990 to 2021, the number of people suffering from depression and anxiety in China rose by 54% and 31.2% respectively. According to the statistics from the 2023 Blue Book on Mental Health in China, the number of psychological counseling institutions has exceeded 100,000, with an average annual growth rate of over 40%. These all highlight the severity of public mental health issues.

China’s Depression and Anxiety Cases in 1990 vs 2021 (million)

This convergence gives rise to a new trend: the healing economy. The healing economy refers to an economic model focusing on people's mental health in urban settings. In practice, it includes some wellness-centered small businesses, such as dog cafés. According to the statistics from DianPing, a platform focused on providing local information on popular new businesses and attractions, the number of dog cafés in Beijing has been increasing year by year, from four in 2019 to nine in 2022, and to 27 in 2025.

Beijing Dog Café Count Trend (2019 – 2025)

The dog café's profits have gone far beyond Wang's expectations. "I was surprised that so many people came on the first week. The six of us servers were run off our feet," Wang said.

The café primarily earns revenue from selling food and drinks to people, but in essence, customers come to satisfy their emotional need. “Once there was a guest who came here alone sadly,” Wang recalled. “He had a drink, played with the dog, and cried it out. After that, his depression was released. There was also a man who came and said that if everyone were given a dog as a present at the weekly meeting, it might make things much more peaceful.”

“Psychologically, this deep-seated emotional need can be classified into three categories,” said Shi Yuan, head of the social psychology program at Tsinghua University. The first category is the need for emotional experience and comfort. With high living and work pressure, office workers often live alone, and the marriage rate has dropped sharply compared to previous years. In such circumstances, dog cafés provide young people with spaces to get accompanied by spending money.

“The intimate interaction between humans and pets can easily trigger the secretion of dopamine, which can immediately bring relaxation and pleasure to people,” said Shi. Peer-reviewed research supports this mechanism: A 2018 study in BMC Psychiatry confirmed that tactile contact with pets rapidly increases dopamine and oxytocin levels—hormones linked to emotional bonding and relaxation (Brooks et al.). “For example, when your hands touch these dogs, you will instantly feel comforted and have the feeling of being accompanied.”

A customer pets the dog inside the café on a rainy day, June 10. Photo by Wu Jiayi.

The second category is the need for relieving anxiety. Due to the high housing prices and high consumption in big cities like Beijing, young people have anxiety and confusion about their future path.

“Going to a dog cafe is an emotional outlet, allowing young people to temporarily escape the pressure of reality and gain a breathing space psychologically,” Shi said.

The third category is the social need. Everyone here has a fondness for dogs, so they are likely to establish connections with others who share the same hobbies. Here they find like-minded companions and gain a sense of belonging. As a result, dog cafés aren’t just about comfort; They also become a place to make social connections, not only for humans, but also for dogs.

“I saw this store on social media and noticed that the dogs here are all golden border collies just like my dog,” said Ma Yaoyang, one customer of the dog café. “So I drove straight over here to let her social and have some fun.”

Ma's dog, Goudan, comes to Two Puppies to find friends, June 10. Photo by Wu Jiayi.

The dog café economy may become more enduring.

“These cafés are a stress-free social setting that doesn't require people to maintain complex interpersonal relationships,” Shi said, “They replace certain functions of interpersonal emotional support, and it is beneficial for the emotional regulation of young people.”

In the future solo economy environment, dog cafés will be a long-term choice for young people. To better adapt to this trend, Wang is making a thorough plan for her café.

“We will establish our own brand 'Two Puppies' and sell pet fresh food and souvenirs in our café,” she said. “Later, we plan to set aside some special events every month, providing a place for dog owners to share their experiences.”

Mizzou Symphony Orchestra Audio Feature

NPR Style Script

ANCHOR INTRO: At the University of Missouri, music isn’t limited to music majors. Students from across campus come together each week to perform as one orchestra. For many of them, it’s more than just rehearsals — it’s a shared community through sound. Jiayi Wu, from J1200 has more/reports

NPR STYLE ASSIGNMENT:
Filename: 1010MUOrchestra TRT: 2:00 SOC

Fade in – natural sound of [the rataplan of the drum and the sound of the horn blowing]

[Jiayi Wu] NARRATION:
It’s a Tuesday evening at the University of Missouri. Inside the rehearsal hall, the University Symphony Orchestra is preparing for another performance. For many students, this isn’t just about perfecting a piece of music, it’s about finding connection through sound.
Dr. John McKeever, Director of Orchestral Activities at Mizzou, says the orchestra brings together students from all parts of campus.

Fade in – natural sound of [rehearsal sounds]

SOUNDBITE 1: Dr. John McKeever TRT: 00:33 – 0:50
“The university symphony orchestra is a 70-person ensemble that meets three times a week and is comprised of both music majors and non-majors from University of Missouri. As we continue, our goal is always to grow both as musicians and as an ensemble.”

[Jiayi Wu] NARRATION:
For Dr McKeever, the orchestra is more than a class. It’s a community where students learn from one another.

SOUNDBITE 2: Dr. John McKeever TRT: 00:57 – 1:15
“One of the great services that the orchestra provide is an opportunity for students to stay active as musicians, so for those of them who are majoring in music they get to apply all the things they’re learning in their theory and their history courses, and for those who are studying something else they have a place to come together and stay active in an art form that they really love.”

[Jiayi Wu] NARRATION:
Among those students is Victoria Cui, a journalism major who once performed in the ensemble. She says the orchestra gave her a sense of belonging that went beyond the classroom.

Fade in – natural sound of [orchestra tone tuning before concert]

SOUNDBITE 3: Victoria Cui TRT: 1:27 – 1:47
“I feel really euphoric. I think it was a great introduction to the community here at Mizzou, because the orchestra is such like a sort of intimate, sort of team, you are bunch together in a room, and you have to play the piece that not only required to play your individual part, but you are an individual as part of a whole entire group.”

[Jiayi Wu] NARRATION:
For both music and non-music majors, the University Symphony Orchestra is a bridge connecting students through shared passion, discipline, and joy.

Fade out – natural sound of [audience applauding after the performance]

(SIGN OFF) From J1200, I’m Jiayi Wu.
